# Career & Engineering Notes

This document contains practical advice for developing as a software engineer.

The goal is not only to become better at writing code, but also to understand how to work effectively in a team, create value, communicate clearly, and continuously improve.

## 1. Be Transparent

Be transparent about your work.

If you are blocked, say so early. If you do not understand something, ask. If you made a mistake, communicate it and focus on fixing it.

Do not hide problems hoping that they will disappear. Good communication gives the team the opportunity to help before a small problem becomes a big one.

## 2. Get Things Done

Technical knowledge is important, but delivering results is equally important.

Learn to:

- Break large problems into smaller tasks.
- Prioritize what matters.
- Finish what you start.
- Communicate when something is blocked.
- Avoid spending too much time perfecting something that does not provide additional value.

A good engineer does not simply produce code. They help move the project forward.

## 3. Be Proactive

Do not always wait for someone to tell you what to do next.

When you finish a task:

- Look at the next related task.
- Check if something can be improved.
- Ask whether anyone needs help.
- Investigate problems you notice.
- Suggest improvements when you have a reasonable idea.

Being proactive does not mean making decisions without communication. It means taking initiative and bringing ideas and problems to the team.

## 4. Be Visible

Make your work visible to the team.

Use the tools and processes the team has agreed on:

- Keep GitLab issues updated.
- Keep your Merge Requests clear.
- Communicate progress and blockers.
- Share relevant findings.
- Explain important technical decisions.

People should not have to guess what you are working on or whether you are blocked.

## 5. Take Ownership

Try to become responsible for something.

It can start small:

- Own a feature.
- Take responsibility for a component.
- Lead an integration.
- Improve documentation.
- Help coordinate a technical task.

Ownership means caring about the result, not just completing the individual task assigned to you.

## 6. Being a Software Engineer Is Not Only Coding

Writing code is only one part of the job.

A software engineer also needs to:

- Understand requirements.
- Communicate with colleagues.
- Understand business problems.
- Participate in discussions.
- Review code.
- Write tests.
- Document decisions.
- Investigate problems.
- Understand systems and dependencies.
- Think about reliability and maintainability.
- Help the team work effectively.

## 7. Think About Quality

Quality applies to much more than code.

Think about the quality of:

- Code
- Tests
- Architecture
- Documentation
- API design
- Git history
- Processes
- Meetings
- Communication
- Collaboration
- Team atmosphere

Also ask:

> What value does this work provide from a business perspective?

A technically elegant solution that solves the wrong problem is still a bad solution.

### Consistency

Consistency is one of the simplest ways to improve the quality and maintainability of a codebase.

When adding or changing code, look at how similar problems are already solved in the project and try to follow the established conventions.

Consistency is often more valuable than personal preference.

This applies to:

- Naming
- Project structure
- API design
- Error handling
- Logging
- Testing
- Code formatting
- Git workflow
- Documentation

Before introducing a new approach, ask:

> Is there already an established way of doing this in the project?

A codebase where everything follows the same conventions is easier to understand, review, maintain, and extend.

## 8. Attend Conferences and Community Events

Attend software engineering conferences, meetups, workshops, and community events when you have the opportunity.

You do not need to understand every talk. The value is in:

- Learning about technologies and ideas outside your daily work.
- Seeing how other engineers approach problems.
- Discovering tools and practices you may not encounter at work.
- Meeting people from the industry.
- Understanding where the industry is heading.
- Getting different perspectives on familiar problems.

Even a single useful idea from a conference can influence how you approach your work.

## 9. Follow People From the Community

Following experienced engineers is a good way to continuously learn.

Some examples:

- **Venkat Subramaniam** – Java expert, author, educator, and frequent conference speaker. His talks often focus on modern Java, programming practices, and writing better code.
- **Vlad Mihalcea** – Java Champion and author known particularly for Java persistence, JPA, Hibernate, SQL, and database performance. His blog contains extensive practical material on Hibernate and persistence. 
- **Josh Long** – Spring Developer Advocate and Java Champion. He creates Spring-focused talks, videos, podcasts, and other educational material.
- **Victor Rentea** – Java/Spring speaker, Java Champion and trainer with a strong focus on practical software development and enterprise Java.

These are only examples. Find people whose way of explaining and thinking helps you learn.

## 10. Java Champions

Java Champions are experienced members of the Java community who have been recognized for their contribution to the Java ecosystem.

They include developers, authors, speakers, educators, open-source contributors, and community leaders.

Following Java Champions can be useful because they often:

- Speak at conferences.
- Publish technical articles.
- Create educational content.
- Contribute to open source.
- Discuss new Java features.
- Share practical lessons from real projects.

Do not follow people simply because they are famous. Follow people whose content helps you understand technology better.

## 11. Follow Technical Blogs and Channels

Do not rely on a single source of information.

Follow several blogs, YouTube channels, newsletters, and technical communities.

For example:

- **TechWorld with Nana** – useful educational content around DevOps, Kubernetes, Docker, cloud technologies, and software engineering.
- **Spring official guides** – practical material for learning Spring and related technologies.
- **Vlad Mihalcea's blog** – particularly useful for JPA, Hibernate, SQL, persistence performance, transactions, and database-related topics.

The important thing is to develop a habit of learning continuously.

## 12. Read Books

You do not need to read dozens of books. A few good books read carefully can have a much greater impact than consuming large amounts of shallow content.

### Habits and Personal Development

**Atomic Habits — James Clear**

A practical book about building good habits and improving gradually through small, consistent changes.

For a software engineer, this can be useful because long-term improvement comes from consistent learning, practice, and reflection.

### Software Engineering

**Clean Code — Robert C. Martin**

A classic introduction to writing readable and maintainable code.

**Clean Architecture — Robert C. Martin**

Focuses on structuring software so that it remains maintainable as requirements and systems evolve.

**Get Your Hands Dirty on Clean Architecture — Tom Hombergs**

A practical book connecting Clean Architecture concepts with real Spring Boot development.

**Effective Java — Joshua Bloch**

A highly recommended Java book covering practical techniques and best practices for writing robust Java code.

**The Pragmatic Programmer — David Thomas and Andrew Hunt**

A broader software engineering book covering habits, problem solving, maintainability, communication, and professional development.

**Designing Data-Intensive Applications — Martin Kleppmann**

A more advanced book covering distributed systems, databases, data processing, replication, partitioning, consistency, and scalability.

**Release It! — Michael T. Nygard**

A practical book about building systems that remain reliable under real-world conditions, including resilience, timeouts, circuit breakers, and failure isolation.

## 13. Keep Learning, but Learn With Purpose

You do not need to learn every new technology.

Instead, try to understand:

- What problem does it solve?
- Why was it created?
- What are its strengths and weaknesses?
- When should it be used?
- When should it not be used?
- What alternatives exist?

Depth in fundamentals is usually more valuable than knowing many technologies superficially.

## 14. Build Your Professional Reputation

Over time, your professional reputation is built through many small things:

- Being reliable.
- Communicating clearly.
- Taking responsibility.
- Helping others.
- Writing quality code.
- Being willing to learn.
- Admitting when you do not know something.
- Following through on commitments.
- Treating colleagues with respect.

Technical skills may get you into a team. Your attitude, reliability, communication, and ability to create value are what make people want to work with you again.

## Final Thought

You do not need to become an expert overnight.

Try to improve a little every day.

Write better code. Ask better questions. Understand the business better. Communicate more clearly. Take responsibility for more things. Learn from people with more experience.

And occasionally step back and ask:

> Am I becoming a better software engineer, or am I only becoming faster at writing code?
